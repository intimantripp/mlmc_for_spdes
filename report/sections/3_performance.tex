\chapter{Performance Evaluation}\label{chap:performance}

Having validated the numerical implementations in the preceding chapter, 
we now present the performance analysis. This chapter addresses 
two of the central questions of this dissertation by quantifying 
the computational savings achieved by the MLMC method relative to the 
standard MC method and assessing the performance 
impact of different coupling strategies.

To this end, we outline the methodology used to measure 
performance. Following the approach detailed by Giles \cite{giles2015multilevel}
and used in the work by Cornalba 
and Fischer \cite{cornalba2025multilevel}, our primary metric for assessing 
theoretical performance is the normalised computational cost. For a range of 
target RMSEs, $\varepsilon$, we plot $\varepsilon^2 \times \mathrm{Cost}$
versus $\varepsilon$ on a log-log scale for both the MC and MLMC estimates. 
This metric is chosen because it 
connects directly to the MLMC Complexity Theorem. An optimal method 
with a theoretical complexity of $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ 
will appear as an approximately horizontal line.

$\mathrm{Cost}$ is determined using a semi-analytical estimate of the computational
work, derived from the number of samples $N_\ell$ used by the 
MLMC Algorithm, and a theoretical cost per sample, $C_\ell \propto 2^{\gamma \ell}$.
We have established that in all our implementations $\gamma = 3$.
We estimate the total MLMC Cost, $C_{\mathrm{MLMC}}$, for a given 
$\varepsilon$, therefore in the following way:

\begin{equation*}
    C_{\mathrm{MLMC}} := \sum_{\ell = 0}^L N_\ell C_\ell = \sum_{\ell = 0}^L N_\ell 2^{3\ell}.
\end{equation*}

For MC estimates, cost is calculated using the relationship between the
variance of the MC estimator and the number of samples taken, namely
$\varepsilon^2 \approx \frac{\mathbb{V}[P_\ell]}{N}$. We can infer 
from the MLMC Algorithm that the most refined level progressed to in order 
to achieve a given RMSE, $L$, 
is sufficient to reduce the discretisation error component of the RMSE, 
following Definition \ref{def:mse_rmse}. Therefore, we can quantify 
the MC Cost, $C_{\mathrm{MC}}$, as

\begin{equation*}
    C_{\mathrm{MC}} = N_{\mathrm{MC}} C_L = \frac{2 \mathbb{V}[P_L]}{\varepsilon^2}2^{3L}
\end{equation*}

where the factor of $2$ is included because in our MLMC Algorithm we 
require the statistical error of the estimator to be $\varepsilon / 2$.
We use our MLMC Algorithm implementation to obtain estimates of 
$\mathbb{V}[P_L]$.

The code used was written in C++20 \cite{cpp20standard}, 
and measurements were obtained running the implementation on Intel Gold 6538Y+ processors
\cite{intel_xeon_gold_6538y}.

Across both QoIs, the FE coupling is decisively superior, while NN and CC are broadly comparable. 
This is consistent with the decay rates we observed in Section 
\ref{sec:stoch_heat_validation}. In Figure 
\ref{fig:she_sq_amp_performance} we observe the optimal 
$\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2})$ scaling, which appears as a straight line on our plots. For 
the energy QoI, we observe the sub-optimal $\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-2}\log^2(\varepsilon))$,
with cost only very slowly rising. This illustrates the benefits of 
faster variance decay.

Figures \ref{fig:she_sq_amp_levels_num} and \ref{fig:energy_levels_num}
demonstrate the tangible results of the faster variance decay, with significantly
fewer samples required to achieve the target accuracy compared to the number 
required by the NN and CC methods.

In contrast, the NN and CC methods are effectively indistinguishable
in both cost and allocations.
Their cost plots show a clear downward slope, which is 
characteristic of the suboptimal case where $\beta < \gamma$. As RMSE decreases, 
we begin to observe significant cost reductions.
At larger RMSEs, the NN and CC methods can result in 
the MLMC estimator being less efficient than the MC estimator. 
This is attributable to the variance reduction not 
scaling sufficiently when only a tiny number of samples/levels 
would suffice for MC.

The speedup factors in Table 
\ref{tab:she_cost_scalings} show, even the suboptimal methods can provide 
significant computational savings over the standard Monte 
Carlo method, particularly when high accuracy is required. The FE method 
provides a further extremely significant improvement over this.

The results clearly demonstrate that the MLMC method 
provides a significant computational advantage over 
standard MC for this problem. As shown in Figure 
\ref{fig:dk_performance}, all three MLMC 
implementations achieve markedly lower costs 
than MC, in line with the efficiency gains predicted by 
MLMC theory.

Performance between coupling mechanisms is very 
similar. NN and CC track each other, while FE follows a slightly different trajectory. 
FE shows a larger cost reduction, although 
given that all methods share the same 
variance decay rate, it is unlikely that this improvement is systematic, 
and more plausibly, it is a consequence of 
estimator stochasticity. Nonetheless, 
the size of the observed reductions, reported in 
Table \ref{tab:dk_cost_scalings}, is striking 
when compared with NN and CC.

Figure \ref{fig:dk_num} shows broadly 
similar sample allocations across all three couplings, with 
FE assigning slightly fewer samples, 
particularly at the smallest RMSEs.


Overall, these findings are consistent with the validation results. 
The DK problem 
does not exhibit the stronger 
correlations achieved by FE coupling in the SHE case, and 
the CC method provides no observable improvement 
over NN as was initially considered it could. 
Instead, all three methods display similar cost scaling.

\section{Parallelisation of MLMC Implementations}

Finally, we investigate what performance improvements are achievable 
when parallelising our MLMC methods. Monte Carlo methods are naturally 
suited to parallel computing. Individual samples are 
independent and can therefore be distributed 
across processors. MLMC 
inherits this parallel structure, with 
opportunities for parallelism across levels, samples, and 
within sample PDE solvers \cite{drzisga2017scheduling}.
In this dissertation, we aim to demonstrate 
a baseline parallel efficiency achievable. 
We therefore adopt a naive but effective parallelisation 
scheme using OpenMP to 
distribute the sampling loops across 
multiple threads, and apply this to our 
energy QoI case for the SHE problem. 
This approach directly parallelises over 
independent samples at each level, 
leaving the MLMC algorithm unchanged. While more 
advanced methods could offer further gains, the design is 
sufficient to illustrate the substantial speedups achievable
in practice. 

We report below the measured wall-clock timings for our 
OpenMP-accelerated implementations for all three coupling 
methods, compared with their serial counterparts. 
All runs were completed on Intel Gold 6538Y+ processors, with 10 trials 
at each RMSE. Parallelised implementations were performed using 8 threads, which
was found to offer substantial performance improvement.

Table \ref{tab:omp_speedups} and Figure \ref{fig:omp_perf_chart}
illustrate the effects of this parallelisation. We observe that 
substantial performance improvements are achievable across 
a range of target RMSEs, with speedups ranging 
from approximately $1.3 \times$ to 
$26 \times$. The NN method exhibits the largest 
gains, but also exhibits the greatest variability. The 
FE method demonstrates the least variability and also 
the smallest reductions, although they are still of 
meaningful magnitudes. The CC method achieves improvements
between these two.

These differences can be understood in terms of algorithmic complexity 
and memory access patterns. The NN method is the computationally
simplest, requiring the fewest memory access and 
floating points operations,
making it the best suited to thread-level parallelism. CC, while
similar, requires twice as many samples to be generated 
per sample and more operations required in constructing 
fine level noise. The FE method 
involves the most complex updates and memory access operations,
as it must generate the correlated noises and resolve these from the 
fine to the coarse level. All of this adds additional overhead 
and limits the speedups achievable under naive 
multithreading.

Overall, these results demonstrate that even a naive OpenMP parallelisation
can deliver meaningful reductions in wall-clock time for MLMC.
While more advanced scheduling and hybrid parallelism approaches could 
extract further gains, the baseline 
results illustrate the further potential parallelism offers in practical
MLMC applications. We highlight however, that the standard, non-parallelised FE 
implementation manages to achieve the target RMSEs 
faster than the OMP NN and CC methods, emphasising the value 
an effective coupling method can have.
